Lesson 0004 · Structure

Script & Shot List

Turn the brief into a timed beat sheet and a shot-by-shot table — the checklist that removes all improvisation from generation.

You have a brief (0001) and a cast (0003). This is the lesson with no GPU — it's you and Claude. And it's the one that most separates an ad from a pretty montage. ~12 min. Your win: a shot list — a table where every row is one image you'll generate, with its prompt already written.

1. Structure is what makes 20 seconds feel like an ad

A spot is not a highlight reel. It has a shape. The reliable one for short brand work:

Hook → Problem → Product → Payoff → CTA

Those are your beats. In 20 seconds that might be 4s / 4s / 5s / 4s / 3s. Decide the beats before any shot, because the beats dictate how many shots you need and what each one must accomplish.

2. Use Claude as your writers' room — but keep the reins

Claude ($20 Pro) is excellent at this and it's the sanctioned writing tool for the mission. Give it everything it needs in one paste:

Here is my brand brief: [paste the 7 lines from Lesson 0001].
Write a [20]s ad as a BEAT SHEET: hook, problem, product, payoff, CTA,
with a duration (seconds) per beat. Terse. Then stop — I'll react before
we do shots.
Why beat sheet first, shots second. If you ask for shots immediately you get improvisation dressed as structure. Lock the beats, push back on them, then expand each beat into shots. One message per stage keeps you in control.

Vary your shot sizes. WS / MS / CU / ECU in deliberate rhythm — not six medium shots in a row. Variety is pacing. And respect the 180° rule so cuts don't flip your hero's screen direction.

4. Two things to decide now (they ripple downstream)

Aspect ratio9:16 (social), 16:9 (web/TV), or 1:1. Pick from your brief — it sets the resolution of every still and clip you generate next.
Shot countMilestone 1 needs the hero visibly the same across ≥4 shots. Aim for 5–7 total so you have cutting choices in Resolve.
Voice: narration, not dialogueIf your ad has a voice, make it a voiceover over visuals — not a hero talking to camera. See the tripwire below.
Lip-sync tripwire — decide this NOW, it shapes your shots. The video step (Wan 2.2 I2V, Lesson 0006) animates motion, not mouth-to-audio. It does not lip-sync. So if you shoot your hero talking to camera and drop a voice over it, the mouth won't match and it will look wrong. The fix is a writing choice, and it's how most AI ads sidestep this:
  • Voiceover (recommended): the voice is narration riding over acting/product shots. Keep the camera off any talking mouth. No lip-sync needed — this is how most real ads work anyway.
  • No dialogue: music + on-screen text/CTA, like most social ads. Zero voice problem.
  • Hero talks to camera: needs a dedicated lip-sync model (Wan 2.2 S2V speech-to-video, or tools like LatentSync — all Spark-fit). Milestone 2 (Lesson 0010) — don't attempt it on your first spot.
Write your script so the voice is narration, and this problem never appears.

5. Your win — the shot list ✍️

Done = a table of 5–7 rows, each with shot #, size, camera move, duration, description, and a written still-image prompt that references your sheet's hero. Durations sum to your target length. Bring it back — we'll pressure-test pacing and the 180° line together.

6. Retrieval check (eyes closed first)

Why write the beat sheet before the shot list?

Beats define the ad's shape and how many shots each stretch needs. Jumping straight to shots produces improvisation, not structure. Lock beats, critique them, then expand.

What does the "still-image prompt" column feed?

Lesson 0005 — keyframe stills. Each row's prompt becomes the exact still you generate and lock before animating.

Minimum shots where the hero must be identical, for milestone 1?

≥4. Aim for 5–7 total for cutting flexibility.
