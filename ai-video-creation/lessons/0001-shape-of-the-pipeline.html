Lesson 0001 · Foundations

The Shape of the Pipeline

Before any model: understand the one rule that separates a brand ad from a pile of pretty clips.

You came in asking "what's the whole process — character sheets, storyline, image-to-video?" Here it is, and here's the single idea that makes it work. This lesson is ~8 minutes. Your win: you'll leave with a one-page brief for a real spot — the thing every later stage is judged against.

1. The whole map, in one breath

Nine stages, left to right. Full detail lives in the pipeline reference — glance at it, don't memorize it.

Brief→ Script→ Character sheet→ Shot list→ Keyframe stills→ Image→Video→ Upscale→ Edit+Sound→ Grade+Deliver

2. The one rule

Resolve consistency on cheap still images first. Only animate frames you've already locked.

Newcomers reach for the exciting part — type a prompt, get a video. Then every clip has a different face, different lighting, a product that morphs. They try to fix it in video, which is the slowest and least controllable step. On your DGX Spark a single clip can take minutes to render — you cannot afford to discover a problem there.

So we invert it. We decide identity and composition on stills (seconds to iterate, fully controllable), lock them as keyframes, and only then hand each locked frame to the video model to animate. Image-to-video, not text-to-video. The character sheet exists for exactly this reason: it's your "casting" — the source of truth every keyframe must match.

Why this is the whole game: "cinematic" and "on-brand" both reduce to consistency — same character, same light, same palette, shot after shot. Three tools buy it: a character LoRA, an IPAdapter reference, and ControlNet for composition — plus a unifying color grade at the end. (All defined in the glossary.) You'll meet each in later lessons.

3. Your two hard constraints (know them cold)

Hardware. DGX Spark fits big models (128GB) but is bandwidth-bound (~273 GB/s). Translation: iterate on stills by day, batch video renders overnight. This isn't a limitation to fight — it's the rhythm the workflow is built around. Every model this course recommends is checked against your Spark in the Spark-fit reference — it must FIT (128GB covers it) and you must know if it's interactive or a batch job. why
Licensing. A brand ad is commercial. FLUX.1 [dev] is non-commercial — never ship it. Use Qwen-Image, FLUX.1 [schnell], or SDXL for images; Wan 2.2 / HunyuanVideo 1.5 / LTX-Video for video. the commercial-safe list

4. Retrieval check (close your eyes first)

Answer out loud, then expand. No peeking — effortful recall is what makes this stick.

Where in the pipeline do you resolve a character's identity — and why not later?

At the character sheet and keyframe (still) stages. Not in video, because video is the slowest and least controllable step; a consistency bug found there is enormously expensive to fix.

Which single model named here must you never use for a brand ad, and why?

FLUX.1 [dev] — its license is non-commercial.

What does your Spark's low memory bandwidth change about how you work?

You iterate interactively on stills and batch the video renders (e.g. overnight) rather than expecting real-time video generation.

5. Your win — write the brief ✍️

This is the real artifact. Fill these seven lines (Claude is great for drafting them — paste this list in). Keep it to one page. Bring it back to me and we'll pressure-test it together.

  1. Brand — real or fictional (a fictional one is totally fine for milestone 1).
  2. Audience — who, in one phrase.
  3. One message — the single thing a viewer should remember. Just one.
  4. Tone — three adjectives (e.g. "warm, confident, understated").
  5. Hero — a recurring character, or the product itself, as the thing we keep consistent.
  6. Format — aspect ratio (9:16 / 16:9 / 1:1) + duration (15 / 20 / 30s).
  7. CTA — what the last 2 seconds ask the viewer to do.
