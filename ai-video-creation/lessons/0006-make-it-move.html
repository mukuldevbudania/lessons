Lesson 0006 · Motion

Make It Move

Animate your locked keyframes with Wan 2.2 — and build the batch/overnight rhythm the Spark's bandwidth forces on you.

You locked a still per shot (0005). Now you hand each locked frame to a video model and get a clip. The discipline here is restraint: you're not generating from scratch, you're adding motion to a frame you already approved. ~12 min. Your win: your first shot, moving — one keyframe animated into a 3–5s clip.

1. Image-to-video, never text-to-video

This is the payoff of the whole "lock first" approach. You use I2V (image-to-video): the locked keyframe is frame one, and a short motion prompt says how it should move. The identity and composition are already baked into the frame — the model only has to animate, not invent. Wan 2.2 I2V

2. Wan 2.2 is your workhorse

Commercial-safe (Apache-2.0), native ComfyUI templates, strong motion and camera moves. Load it from Workflow → Browse Templates → Video → "Wan2.2 14B I2V". official native workflow

This is where the Spark earns its keep: Wan 14B at 720p needs 65–80 GB — it's ruled out on every consumer GPU, but your 128 GB loads it fine. Fit isn't the issue; speed is, so you batch. Full table: Spark-fit reference.

3. The Spark rhythm — this is the lesson's real idea

Fits but slow. 128 GB means Wan 14B loads fine; ~273 GB/s bandwidth means each clip takes real minutes. So the workflow is not interactive scrubbing. It's: iterate motion prompts on cheap/low-res or the 5B model by day, then queue your final 14B renders as an overnight batch. why batch
Two speed levers. (1) Generate at 480p first, upscale later (Lesson 0007) — don't pay for 1080p on a take you might reject. (2) A Lightning / fast LoRA cuts step count dramatically (e.g. a handful of steps instead of 20+) for drafts; drop it for the final render if you want maximum quality. fast I2V with Lightning LoRA

4. Writing a motion prompt

Describe motion and camera, not content (the content is already in the frame). Pull the camera move straight from your shot-list row:

slow push in on the hero, subtle breathing, hair drifts,
shallow depth of field, cinematic, 24fps

Keep motions small for a first pass — big motion is where identity drifts and limbs mangle. Short clips (3–5s) that cut well beat one long wandering take.

5. Your win — one shot, moving ✍️

1
Load the Wan2.2 I2V template. Set your locked keyframe as the input image.
2
Write a small motion prompt from that shot's camera move. Generate at 480p, short duration.
3
Judge: did identity hold? Is the motion believable? If yes, queue the rest as a batch and let the Spark run.
Done = one keyframe turned into a short clip where your hero still looks like your hero and the motion matches the shot's intent. That proves the I2V path end-to-end; the remaining shots are just the same recipe, batched.

6. Retrieval check (eyes closed first)

Why I2V instead of T2V for this pipeline?

The keyframe already encodes approved identity + composition. I2V only animates it, so the model can't re-roll the hero's face or the framing. T2V would reinvent everything each clip.

What's the Spark-imposed working rhythm, and why?

Iterate motion on fast/low-res (5B, 480p, Lightning LoRA) by day; batch final 14B renders overnight. Because the Spark is bandwidth-bound — clips are minutes, not seconds, so interactive scrubbing isn't viable.

Why keep first-pass motions small?

Large motion is where identity drifts and anatomy breaks. Small, well-cut motion looks more cinematic and fails less.
