Lesson 0006 · Record Intermediate

Record a Screen + Voiceover Take

Your script is done, so now you capture it — a clean screen recording and clear voiceover are 90% of a good faceless video, and OBS does both for free.

This is a hands-on lesson, about 8 minutes to read and maybe 20 to do your first test. No camera needed — this is the faceless path: OBS captures your screen and your mic, you talk over what you're doing. Your win: you'll have OBS configured correctly, know how to set audio levels so you don't clip, and run a 20-second test take before you burn energy on the real recording.

1. Install OBS and set the output format once

OBS Studio is the standard free, open-source recorder — it's what most faceless creators use. Install it, then set a few project defaults once and never think about them again. These are your "build settings": get them right at the start and every take is consistent.

  1. Download and install OBS Studio from the official site (links in the source callout below). It's on Windows, macOS, and Linux.
  2. Open Settings → Video. Set Base (Canvas) Resolution and Output (Scaled) Resolution both to 1920×1080. Set Common FPS to 30 — fine for talking-over-code; use 60 only for fast gameplay or smooth motion.
  3. Open Settings → Output. Switch Output Mode to Advanced, go to the Recording tab, and set Recording Format to MKV (it survives a crash without corrupting the file — you can remux to MP4 after).
  4. Open Settings → Audio and confirm your Mic/Auxiliary Audio is set to your actual microphone, not "Default."
In your world These settings are your .env and build config. You set resolution, fps, and format once, commit them, and every recording inherits the same known-good configuration. Fiddling with them per-take is like editing production config by hand on every deploy — it's how you end up with one video at the wrong resolution.

2. Add your capture sources

OBS is built around sources — each is a layer in your scene, like components composed into a page. For a faceless screen tutorial you need two: one for picture, one for sound.

In the Sources panel click + → choose Display Capture (whole monitor) or Window Capture (one app only) → name it → OK. Then click + again → Audio Input Capture → pick your mic → OK.

Prefer Window Capture when you can — it records just your editor or browser and won't accidentally leak a Slack notification or a second monitor into frame. Use Display Capture only when you need to move between apps during the take. Either way, close chat apps and silence notifications before you hit record.

3. Set audio levels so you never clip

This is the one step beginners skip and regret. In OBS's Audio Mixer, each source has a level meter. While talking at your normal recording volume, watch the meter and aim for peaks in the yellow zone, roughly −12 dB to −6 dB. If it hits the far right (0 dB / red), you're clipping — the audio distorts permanently and no edit can fix it. Drag the source's gain/volume slider down until your loudest words stay out of the red.

  • Too quiet (meter barely moves, peaks below −30 dB): raise gain — quiet audio you boost later also boosts the hiss.
  • Clipping (peaks slam into red): lower gain, or back off the mic a few inches.
  • Just right: normal speech lives around −18 to −12 dB, loudest peaks under −6 dB with headroom to spare.
In your world Clipping is an integer overflow for sound. The signal exceeds the ceiling the format can represent, so it wraps into garbage — and just like an overflow in production, you can't reconstruct the real value afterward. Setting levels with headroom is defensive coding: leave room below the max so a loud laugh doesn't corrupt the sample.

4. Voiceover: separate track or live-over?

You have two ways to get narration onto a screen recording, and the choice is a real trade-off:

ApproachHowBest when
Live voiceoverTalk while you screen-record, in one pass.Casual walkthroughs, you're comfortable narrating on the fly, you want speed.
Separate trackRecord silent screen capture first, then record narration over the footage in your editor.Tight scripts, complex demos, or when you fumble the on-the-fly take and want clean audio.

Start with live voiceover — it's faster and good enough for your first videos. Move to a separate track later when you want polish: it lets you re-record a muffed sentence without re-recording the screen. Either way, a few room basics help: record in a small soft space (a closet of clothes beats a bare office), keep the mic 6–8 inches from your mouth and slightly off-axis to dodge plosive pops, and stop your HVAC/fan for the take.

Primary source Install, source setup, and audio details come from OBS Studio's own documentation: OBS Studio — official site and the OBS Help & Resources. Trust these over any recording-tips blog.

5. Always do a 20-second test take first

Before the real recording, hit Start Recording, talk for 20 seconds while clicking around as you will in the real take, then Stop and play it back with headphones. Check three things: audio is clear and un-clipped, the right window/screen is captured, and no notification or private tab snuck into frame. Fix anything now — a 20-second test costs nothing; discovering your mic was muted after a 15-minute take costs the whole afternoon.

In your world The test take is your smoke test in staging. You don't ship a release straight to prod without one request confirming the thing boots — you don't commit to a full recording without 20 seconds confirming video and audio actually captured. Cheap check, catches the expensive failures.

6. Retrieval check (answer out loud first)

What dB range should your voice peak at, and what happens if it hits 0 dB?

Aim for peaks around −12 to −6 dB (the yellow zone). Hitting 0 dB means clipping — the audio distorts permanently and can't be repaired in editing.

When would you record a separate voiceover track instead of narrating live?

For tightly scripted or complex demos, or when you fumble the live take — a separate track lets you re-record narration without re-recording the screen footage.

Why run a 20-second test take before the real recording?

To confirm audio is clear and un-clipped, the correct window/screen is captured, and nothing private is in frame — catching a muted mic or wrong source cheaply before you invest in a full take.
